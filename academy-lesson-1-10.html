Further GMP quality-system topics

Foundational GMP includes supplier oversight, complaint handling, recalls, periodic product quality review, cross-contamination prevention and distinct QA and QC responsibilities. This introductory course identifies these as topics for further study rather than claiming comprehensive coverage.

Regulatory references for further reading

EU GMP Chapter 9 specifically addresses self-inspection. US 21 CFR 211.180(e) addresses periodic product-quality evaluation; it is not a standalone US requirement for an identical self-inspection programme.

EU GMP Volume 4 (Chapter 9) · 21 CFR 211.180

Reference links are starting points for the applicable legal or guidance text. Check current versions and jurisdiction-specific applicability; this training is not a regulatory determination.

Readable guide to the video diagrams

Text descriptions of the main slide relationships, in presentation order. Use these with the narration transcript and companion reading. Arrows summarize relationships; they do not replace site procedures or prescribe a universal workflow. The repeated right-hand panels are reminders, not additional mandatory steps.

  1. Internal oversight: review, identify gaps and improve before quality is affected.
  2. Inspection plan: define scope, prioritize and assign objectively.
  3. Prepare evidence: procedures, records and current status must reflect real operations.
  4. Conduct inspection: observe, interview and document evidence rather than assumptions.
  5. Findings: record facts, impact and follow-up, separating verified findings from advice.
  6. Corrective action: establish cause, act and verify effectiveness.
  7. Daily readiness: train, record and review continuously.
  8. Expired-status example: secure, assess and resolve; replacing a label alone is insufficient.

00:00:00 Self-inspection is a systematic internal review

00:00:02 of whether pharmaceutical operations follow

00:00:05 Good Manufacturing Practice and the company quality system. It helps identify

00:00:10 weaknesses before they affect patients or product quality. A self-inspection program

00:00:15 should cover relevant people, premises,

00:00:17 equipment, operations and records. The purpose

00:00:20 is to find and correct genuine problems, not simply to produce

00:00:25 a favorable score.

00:00:27 A useful self-inspection program defines scope,

00:00:30 responsibilities, frequency and follow-up. Planning

00:00:33 can consider process criticality, past deviations,

00:00:37 previous findings and changes. Inspectors

00:00:40 should be sufficiently knowledgeable and objective for the activity. Risk-based prioritization

00:00:45 helps allocate attention, but it does not justify omitting required GMP

00:00:50 controls. The plan should be documented under the site quality system.

00:00:55 Before an inspection, identify the approved procedures and records relevant to

00:00:59 the selected area. Examples may include training records, equipment logs, cleaning

00:01:05 records, deviations, change controls and

00:01:08 qualification status. Inspectors should check the

00:01:11 current approved version and whether records reflect actual work. A tidy

00:01:16 folder alone does not demonstrate that an operation is controlled. Evidence

00:01:20 must be reliable and traceable.

00:01:23 During the inspection, observe work, interview

00:01:25 personnel and compare actual practices

00:01:28 with approved requirements. Follow the process from material or personnel entry

00:01:32 through operations and recording. Ask neutral questions and document facts, not

00:01:38 assumptions. When a possible issue is found, capture enough objective evidence

00:01:43 to allow fair assessment. Escalate any immediate product quality or patient

00:01:48 safety concern through the site procedure.

00:01:51 Inspection findings should clearly describe the

00:01:53 observed condition, applicable requirement or

00:01:56 procedure, and supporting evidence. The organization should assess the significance and

00:02:01 potential product impact of each finding.

00:02:04 Classification terminology varies between quality

00:02:07 systems and authorities; do not assign a universal severity label without

00:02:12 an applicable framework. Reports should distinguish

00:02:15 verified observations from recommendations and

00:02:18 should assign accountable follow-up.

00:02:20 Appropriate corrective action should address the causes of confirmed deficiencies, not

00:02:25 just the visible symptom. Action plans need responsible owners and justified

00:02:30 timelines. Some issues require investigation,

00:02:34 product impact assessment, preventive measures or

00:02:36 training. Follow-up should verify implementation and, where appropriate, effectiveness. Closing a

00:02:43 finding solely because a document was signed does not prove that

00:02:47 the underlying problem is resolved.

00:02:49 Inspection readiness is sustained through routine GMP compliance, not a last-minute

00:02:55 cleanup before an audit. Current procedures, complete records, trained personnel and

00:03:01 equipment status controls help teams explain

00:03:04 and demonstrate their processes. Management

00:03:07 review and trend analysis can reveal recurring weaknesses. Personnel should answer

00:03:12 accurately, acknowledge uncertainty and retrieve

00:03:15 evidence through approved systems. Never fabricate

00:03:19 or backdate records to satisfy an inspector.

00:03:22 Consider an internal inspection of a sterile manufacturing support area. An

00:03:28 inspector notices that an equipment calibration label is expired while a

00:03:33 nearby logbook is incomplete. The team should secure the equipment as

00:03:37 required, verify the actual calibration and use status, and assess potential

00:03:42 impact on operations or product quality. The observation and decisions should

00:03:47 be documented and escalated. A new label alone is not an

00:03:52 adequate corrective action without confirming the underlying status.
