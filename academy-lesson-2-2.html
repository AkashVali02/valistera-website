Part 1 of 4

A map, not a pile of protocols

View technical information · Diagram 1

Lesson explanation

A useful validation master plan helps a new team member understand how qualification and validation are governed. In European Union GMP Annex 15, the programme may be defined in a VMP or equivalent document. This is not a requirement to give every site the same template. Our worked plan is a teaching structure. The applicable quality system determines the authorized document structure and approval responsibilities.

Part 2 of 4

Make scope visible

View technical information · Diagram 2

Lesson explanation

For our small oral-liquid project, the plan links the mixing vessel, transfer equipment, utilities and supporting procedures. A scope table records the system identifier, intended use, responsible owner and current evidence status. An entry called complete without a report reference is difficult to challenge. An explicit status such as design review pending tells the team what still needs to happen. A date in a schedule is not proof of qualification.

Part 3 of 4

Separate the documents

View technical information · Diagram 3

Lesson explanation

The programme plan explains the approach and oversight. A protocol states how a particular verification will be performed and judged. A report evaluates what actually happened. The diagram presents these as connected documents rather than interchangeable files. For example, the plan may require risk-informed testing; the protocol specifies a particular mixing test; the report evaluates the observed results and any deviations.

Part 4 of 4

Review the living programme

View technical information · Diagram 4

Lesson explanation

Now suppose the project adds a second product with different cleaning needs. Reusing the original plan unchanged may conceal a new scope. Compare the new use with the approved assumptions, identify the affected systems, and route updates through the quality system. In the activity, prepare a short programme outline with owners, document references and review triggers. A useful plan should help people act, not merely describe intentions.

Required reading: VMP coverage

Annex 15 §1.4 says programme elements should be documented in a VMP or equivalent; the recorded phrase “may be defined” refers to the document choice, not an option to omit documentation.

Include policy, responsibilities, system/status summary, change and deviation handling, acceptance-criteria guidance, document references and strategy including applicable requalification (§1.5). Requalification evaluates continued control (§4).

Worked V-201 outline: policy—approved, risk-based qualification; owner—validation engineer with Quality oversight; status—DQ pending, installation not released; deviations—log and investigate failed criteria; changes—evaluate effects on URS and evidence; criteria—process owner provides scientific basis before approval; references—URS-V201, RA-V201, drawings; strategy—stage gates and periodic/change-triggered review of continued control. Each item needs an actual controlled reference in a real project.

Required reading: name the qualification stages

URS and/or functional specification → DQ → applicable FAT/SAT → IQ → OQ → PQ. This is a learning map, not a mandatory identical sequence for every project. Document justified combinations and applicability.

DQ verifies the URS against the design. FAT/SAT examine supplier/site acceptance. IQ checks installation; OQ checks operation and limits; PQ checks intended performance. Keep the URS as the lifecycle reference (Annex 15 §§3.1–3.14).

For V-201, trace URS-02 through design drawing DR-02, applicable factory checks, installed sensor identification, operational accuracy testing, and performance evidence under the intended load. A supplier pass alone does not close every stage.
