Part 1 of 4

The review packet

Key explanation · Part 1

You are reviewing the fictional V-201 handover. The intended use is documented and the material records are available. However, the required mixing range has not been finalized, the transfer-hose cleaning evidence is missing and the alarm response has not been verified. A dashboard says 90% complete. Your task is to decide what the evidence supports, not to maximize the completion percentage.

Part 2 of 4

Separate project progress from readiness

Key explanation · Part 2

A percentage can be useful for scheduling, but it treats unlike activities as if they were interchangeable. Finishing several minor tasks does not offset an unresolved requirement that affects product quality. The visual compares a large completion bar with three named gaps. Read the gaps directly: mixing range, cleaning evidence and alarm response. Their significance must be evaluated individually in the context of the intended use.

Part 3 of 4

Write a reasoned decision

Key explanation · Part 3

For this exercise, routine-use readiness is not demonstrated. State that conclusion, identify the missing evidence, assign owners and define what must be reviewed before a new decision. If a project proposes progression to a limited next stage, assess that specific activity and its dependencies separately through the approved system. Do not describe conditional progression as general permission for production. The scope of an authorization must be unambiguous.

Worked artefact: two different handover decisions

Additional fictional case facts: installation identification and electrical safety checks are complete. Transfer-hose cleaning evidence and product mixing range remain open. A separate, approved dry control-panel simulation uses no product, water or transfer hose. Its hazard review finds no interaction with those gaps. The responsible site reviewers have not yet signed its stage authorization.

Now change one fact: the proposed next activity uses the transfer hose. Explain why the same conditional decision no longer holds. A defensible answer names the specific activity, dependencies and authority—not merely “conditional”.
